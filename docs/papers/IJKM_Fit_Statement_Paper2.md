# Statement of Fit — Paper 2 → International Journal of Knowledge Management (IJKM)

(≈470 words; for the submission form / cover letter)

This manuscript is appropriate for the International Journal of Knowledge
Management because it addresses, with original empirical evidence, the
question at the centre of the journal's stated mission: the organizational,
technical, human and cognitive issues associated with the creation, capture,
transfer and use of knowledge — and, specifically, why a well-engineered
knowledge system can fail at the point of human reception.

The problem addressed is a knowledge-transfer failure with real industrial
and social cost. A clinically validated Fall Risk Assessment (FRA) system
is, in knowledge-management terms, a knowledge infrastructure: it captures
balance telemetry, converts it into clinical knowledge, and returns to the
elderly user actionable knowledge about their own body. Yet at the study
site only 39% of residents had ever used the installed system, and most who
failed at first use never returned. Our eight-week action-research
deployment of a gamified, AI-assisted FRA with thirty elderly residents in
a Southern China care facility documents the failure precisely: 70% of
participants disengaged at or before three game cycles, none re-engaged,
and — most strikingly — participants whose recorded balance index
measurably improved during their only session still permanently abandoned
the system. The knowledge was created, captured and delivered; it was the
acceptance of that knowledge that collapsed.

The significance and novelty are twofold. Theoretically, the manuscript
contributes Failure-Induced Abandonment (FIAS), a provisional behavioural
construct that explains knowledge rejection through self-efficacy collapse
and loss aversion: the elderly recipient declines further knowledge about
themselves because its anticipated identity cost exceeds its perceived
benefit. This is a cognitive dimension of knowledge use that the prevailing
acceptance models (TAM, UTAUT, STAM) and gamification frameworks
(Octalysis) do not represent, and the paper supplies four falsifiable
predictions through which the construct can be tested. An
information-entropy lens additionally models the recipient's belief state
before and after failure, connecting the behavioural finding to knowledge
representation. Practically, the manuscript contributes an implemented
knowledge system utilizing artificial intelligence — squarely within the
journal's scope — in which a large-language-model adaptation layer runs on
existing clinical infrastructure, together with codified practitioner
knowledge that care organizations can apply directly: operational
definitions, observable abandonment-risk signals, a staff coaching
specification, and design principles for framing knowledge delivery so
that it does not threaten the recipient's identity.

The manuscript belongs in IJKM because it advances the journal's core
concern from an under-examined direction. Most knowledge-management
research asks how knowledge is created, codified and shared; this study
asks what happens when the knowledge a system delivers is unwelcome to the
person it concerns — and shows, in a population for whom the stakes of
knowledge acceptance are highest, that reception is governed by identity
and loss, not by access or usability. Building on gamification-for-
knowledge-transfer research in ageing populations, it offers the journal's
readership rare field evidence, a testable construct, and deployable
design guidance in a single empirical study.
